The GST rounding mistake that costs tradies an argument a month

There are two ways to work out the GST on an invoice, they disagree by a cent or two, and only one of them is what the ATO expects.

The two ways

Line by line. Work out 10% of each line, round each to the nearest cent, then add the lot up.

On the total. Add up the lines, then take 10% of that number and round once.

Most of the time they agree. When they don't, you get an invoice where the GST line is one cent off what the customer's bookkeeper calculated — and one cent is enough to have the invoice sent back, which costs you a fortnight of payment terms.

A worked example

Three lines, all GST-inclusive pricing aside, ex-GST amounts:

  • Supply and install tap set — $186.35
  • Labour, 2.5 hours — $237.50
  • Callout — $88.15

Line by line: $18.64 (rounded up from 18.635) + $23.75 + $8.82 (rounded up from 8.815) = $51.21.

On the total: $512.00 × 10% = $51.20.

One cent. Now put that on a $40,000 progress claim with sixty lines and the gap gets wider and harder to explain.

Which one is right

The ATO's rounding rules say that if you have more than one taxable sale on an invoice, you work out the GST on each and add them — the "total invoice rule" only applies when you're taking 1/11th of a single total, and even then you round at the end. Line by line is the safe answer, and it's the answer your customer's accounting software will almost always produce, because that's what it does too.

How to check your own software in thirty seconds

Make a quote with two lines that both end in a half cent of GST — $186.35 and $88.15 will do it. If the GST comes out to $27.46, it's rounding per line. If it says $27.45, it's rounding on the total, and one day it will cost you.
